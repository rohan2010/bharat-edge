The monsoon reaches the southern coast of India in early June and moves north over the following weeks. For farmers, its timing decides when to plant rice, cotton and pulses. A late or weak monsoon can mean lower harvests, higher food prices and more debt for families who depend on a single crop. A strong monsoon brings its own problems: rivers overflow, roads wash away and cities with poor drainage flood within hours.

Weather scientists study ocean temperatures, wind patterns and pressure systems to forecast the season. Their forecasts have improved a great deal over the last thirty years, but rainfall in any one district is still hard to predict. Many villages now receive short text messages that tell them when heavy rain is expected, and some state governments use these warnings to move people away from rivers before a flood.

In the cities, the first rain of the season is a small celebration. Children run outside, tea stalls are crowded, and the smell of wet earth fills the streets. By August the mood changes. Traffic crawls through waterlogged roads, trains run late, and people start to wait for the clear skies of October. Then the cycle begins again the next year, as it has for as long as anyone can remember.

Schools often close for a few days when the rain is heaviest. Teachers send homework by phone, and parents try to keep their children busy indoors. Shopkeepers cover their goods with plastic sheets, and street vendors move under bridges and shop awnings. Electricity can fail for hours at a time, so many families keep candles, torches and a charged phone ready. Doctors warn that standing water breeds mosquitoes, and every year hospitals prepare for more cases of dengue and malaria in the weeks after the heaviest rain.
